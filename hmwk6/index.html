Theoretical Background

Concept of Independence

In probability, two random variables X and Y are statistically independent if the occurrence of one does not affect the probability of the other. Mathematically:

P(X ∩ Y) = P(X) × P(Y)

In the context of frequency data, this implies that the conditional distributions of one variable are identical across all categories of the other variable.

Chi-Square Test (χ²)

To test for independence, we compare Observed frequencies (O) with the Expected frequencies (E) calculated assuming independence.

χ² = Σ [ (O - E)² / E ]

Large χ² values indicate a significant deviation from independence. The test uses degrees of freedom: df = (Rows - 1) × (Cols - 1).

Association vs. Causality

A statistically significant Chi-Square test only proves association (the variables are dependent), it does not imply causation. "Correlation does not imply causation." The observed relationship might be due to a third, unmeasured factor known as a confounding variable. Establishing causality requires controlled experiments and rigorous study designs, not just statistical tests on observational data.
