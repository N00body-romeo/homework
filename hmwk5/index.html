Riemann vs Lebesgue Integration & Normal CDF Computation

Exercise 1: Riemann and Lebesgue Integration

Theoretical Background: The Riemann integral approximates the area under a curve by partitioning the domain (x-axis) into small subintervals and summing the areas of vertical rectangles. The Lebesgue integral, conversely, partitions the range (y-axis) into subsets and measures the size (Lebesgue measure) of the preimage in the domain.

For well-behaved functions (continuous, piecewise continuous), both methods converge to the same value. However, for "pathological" functions like the Dirichlet function, Riemann integration fails because the function oscillates infinitely within any domain interval. Lebesgue integration handles this elegantly because it relies on the measure of sets (e.g., the measure of rationals is 0).

Exercise 2: Normal CDF Computation

Why Numerical Methods?

According to Liouville's Theorem, the antiderivative of functions like e-x² cannot be expressed in terms of a finite combination of elementary functions (polynomials, exponentials, logarithms, trigonometric functions). Because the probability density function (PDF) of the Normal distribution relies on e-x²/2, its cumulative distribution function (CDF) has no closed-form elementary solution.

To handle this, mathematicians introduced the Error Function (erf), providing an analytical representation:
Φ(z) = 0.5 * [1 + erf(z / √2)]

However, erf(x) itself is a non-elementary special function, meaning practically, we must rely on numerical methods (integration, Taylor series, or rational approximations) to compute probabilities.
